01 · SYMBOLIC AI

A logic engine that sees chess motifs.

Strong players recognise patterns such as a bishop pair, a pin, or a bad bishop at a glance. My logic engine makes that knowledge explicit: each motif is defined as a logical rule over pieces, squares, and the relations between them.

How it works

  1. Facts. A position is translated into facts: which piece stands on which square, what each piece attacks and defends, and how mobile it is.
  2. Rules. Each motif is a rule that combines these facts. If the conditions of a rule hold, the motif is present.
  3. Explanation. Because the rules are explicit, the engine can show exactly which squares form the motif and why it matters, unlike a black-box evaluation.

Each rule is a simple checklist. If every item on the list is true, the engine has found that pattern on the board. :- means "if" and a comma means "and". The grey text explains each line.

IN DEVELOPMENT · INDUCTIVE LOGIC PROGRAMMING

From writing rules to learning them.

Hand-written rules only capture what I already know. With inductive logic programming (ILP), the program compares chess positions and learns the rules itself, including new concepts that nobody gave it.

Learned rule

A rule that covers the positive examples and none of the negative ones.

motif(B) :-
    bishop(B, S),
    inv_1(B, P),
    pawn(P, S).

inv_1(B, P) :-
    on_diagonal(B, P),
    blocks(P, B).

Predicate invention

The most interesting part is predicate invention: the learner can create helper concepts, such as inv_1 above, that do not exist in its background knowledge. Here it has invented something like "a pawn blocking the bishop's diagonal", which a human would call a key idea behind a bad bishop.

Why it matters

If a program can invent the same concepts that chess players use, it suggests how such concepts might be learned from experience. If it invents different ones, it may reveal patterns that players have never named.

02 · COGNITIVE ARCHITECTURE

CHREST: how experts remember positions.

CHREST (Chunk Hierarchy and REtrieval STructures), developed by Fernand Gobet and colleagues, is a computational model of human perception, learning, and memory. It explains one of the best-known findings in expertise research: masters can reproduce a game position after a few seconds, but not a random one.

Chunks

Familiar groups of pieces, such as a castled king with its pawns, learned through experience.

Long-term memory

A discrimination network that grows as the model sees more positions. Experts hold many large chunks.

Short-term memory

Holds only about four chunks. Bigger chunks mean more pieces remembered in the same space.

Templates

Frequently seen chunks become templates with slots, which let experts encode positions even faster.

    The expert's advantage comes from recognising large, familiar chunks. In a random position there is nothing familiar to recognise, so the expert does no better than the novice. This mirrors the results of Chase and Simon (1973) that CHREST was built to explain. This demo was written for this page and is not the CHREST software itself.

    What I am working on

    I use CHREST to model how chess players perceive and memorise positions, and to compare its behaviour with data from my experiments on expertise and the Einstellung effect.

    The open question

    CHREST learns which groups of pieces belong together, but not what they mean. My logic engine knows what motifs mean, but not how they are learned. I am exploring how to combine the two, so that perceptual chunks can be linked to explicit, explainable concepts.

    03 · WORK IN PROGRESS · NEURAL NETWORKS

    Same experience, same rating: human or machine?

    MAIA is a neural chess engine trained to play like humans of a specific rating, rather than to play perfectly. I am using it to ask a sharper question: if a model and a human share the same rating and the same chess experience, do they think alike?
