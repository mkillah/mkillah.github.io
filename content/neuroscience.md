# Neuroscience

*Brain · Perception · Expertise*

## What I have been researching

So far I have studied the mind mainly at the **behavioural level**: how people with different levels of expertise perceive, remember, and solve problems. Eye tracking has given me the closest window into the underlying processes, showing where attention goes and how visual information is taken in before a decision is made.

### Research themes

- **Visual attention.** How eye movements reveal what people notice in a complex scene, and how this differs between experts and novices.
- **Perception and recognition.** How the brain turns visual input into meaningful patterns that can be recognised at a glance.
- **Expertise and the brain.** How long-term practice in a domain changes the way information is processed.

I researched these topics in both my bachelor's and master's theses.

## Where I am heading

The directions below are my research interests within **neuroscience**. My interests in psychology, artificial intelligence, and linguistics have their own focus, but a cognitive science approach unites them all in one goal: **investigating the human mind by bringing together skills and knowledge from different fields.**

Within neuroscience, my next step is to connect what people do to **how the brain makes it possible**, linking my behavioural findings on expertise to their neural basis.

### Expertise and individual differences

Experts recognise meaningful patterns almost instantly, and people differ widely in perceptual and cognitive abilities. I want to understand which brain processes support expert performance, how they change as expertise develops, and how individual differences relate to differences in brain structure and function. Expert populations offer a measurable range of skill for studying this, building on my behavioural research.

### Pattern recognition and memory

I am interested in how the brain recognises familiar patterns and links them to knowledge stored in memory, and how this process allows experts to encode and recall complex information so efficiently.

### Visual agnosias

Visual agnosias, such as the inability to recognise objects or faces despite intact vision, show what happens when recognition breaks down. Studying them reveals how recognition is organised in the brain, and offers a contrast to the exceptional recognition abilities of experts.

### Brain and computational models

Computational models of cognition make precise predictions about processing. I want to test whether these predictions match neural data, connecting cognitive models with measurements of brain activity.

## Methods

### Methods I have used

#### Eye tracking

Recording where and for how long people look, as a measure of visual attention and information processing.

#### Experimental design

Controlled experiments comparing groups that differ in expertise, with manipulations of how information is presented.

#### Statistical analysis

SPSS, R, and Python for data preparation, statistical analysis, and visualisation.

### Methods I want to develop

#### EEG

Measuring the timing of brain activity during perception and decision making, with millisecond precision.

#### fMRI

Identifying the brain networks involved in recognition, memory, and expert problem solving.

#### Physiological measures

Measures such as pupil size and heart rate as indicators of mental effort, arousal, and emotional processing during problem solving.

#### Computational neuroscience

Building and testing models that connect cognitive processes with neural mechanisms.
