# Artificial intelligence

*Cognitive architectures · Knowledge representation · Models of expertise*

## Theoretical interest

### What I have been researching

My interest in artificial intelligence comes from cognitive science: I want to understand human thinking well enough to build working models of it. The question I care about is not only whether a system can solve a problem, but whether it solves it *the way people do*, including the way people make mistakes.

My starting point is **knowledge representation**: how knowledge is structured, stored, and retrieved, and how that structure supports or limits reasoning. Chess is a natural test case. Decades of research show that experts do not simply calculate more; they recognise meaningful patterns and draw on large, well-organised memories of positions.

This leads me to **CHREST** (Chunk Hierarchy and REtrieval STructures), a computational model of expertise in which knowledge is learned as "chunks" and larger "templates" that link perception, memory, and problem solving. CHREST has been used to simulate how chess players perceive and remember positions, which makes it a strong bridge between my experimental work and computational modelling.

### Where I am heading

My aim is to use computational models such as CHREST to explain the patterns I have found in my experiments with chess players:

- why expertise makes people faster and more accurate within their domain,
- why it does not transfer to tasks outside that domain, and
- why familiar patterns can block better solutions (the Einstellung effect).

A model that reproduces both the strengths and the blind spots of human experts would say something important about how expertise is represented in the mind.

## Methods

#### Theoretical analysis

Comparing cognitive architectures and models of expertise, with a focus on chunking and template theory, to judge which mechanisms can account for experimental findings.

#### Computational modelling

Working with CHREST to represent chess knowledge as chunks and templates, and to compare model behaviour with human data on perception, memory, and problem solving.

#### Linking models to experiments

Using behavioural data from my chess studies, such as accuracy, optimal-solution rates, and response times, as targets that a model should reproduce.
